## **Abstract**

A clinically distinctive subset of immune-checkpoint-inhibitor (ICI) hepatitis is refractory to corticosteroids (steroid-resistant; SR-irHep) and progresses towards bile-duct injury and paucity, yet second-line mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is chosen empirically, without an SR-irHep-specific rationale. By 10× Xenium spatial transcriptomics of steroid-exposed SR-irHep liver biopsies (146,014 cells; 380-gene panel), with treatment-naïve type I autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) as comparator (n=2 each), we identify a bile-duct–centred CD8 architecture engaging MHC-I and CXCR3 in SR-irHep, contrasting with parenchyma-centred CD4 costimulation (CD86, ICOSLG–CD28, CD40LG–CD40) and CCR1/4 chemokine signalling in AIH. This axis offers a mechanistic hypothesis for the empirical use of MMF, aligns most closely with JAK1/2 inhibition (ruxolitinib) among reported second-line options, and suggests CXCR3 as a candidate second-line target.

**Keywords:** steroid-resistant ICI hepatitis, vanishing bile-duct syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis, mycophenolate, ruxolitinib, spatial transcriptomics, CXCR3, cell–cell communication.

## **Main text**

Immune-checkpoint-inhibitor (ICI) hepatitis is one of the most frequent dose-limiting immune-related adverse events of PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 blockade, affecting 5–10% of patients on monotherapy and up to ~13% on combination regimens (all grades), with 1–9% at grade ≥3 ^1,2^. Most cases respond to corticosteroids, but ~16% meet criteria for steroid-refractory ICI hepatitis (SR-irHep)^3^, and a clinically distinctive subset progresses to cholangiocyte injury and bile-duct paucity^4^, a vanishing-bile-duct phenotype, formally defined as loss of interlobular bile ducts in >50% of portal tracts, overlapping with ICI-induced secondary sclerosing cholangitis^5^ and distinct from the parenchyma-centred, lymphoplasmacytic picture of classical autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)^6^. Mycophenolate mofetil is the most commonly used empirical second-line agent in SR-irHep^7^, with tacrolimus, ATG and tocilizumab reported in small series^7^. No biology-driven second-line option has yet been established. We compared steroid-exposed SR-irHep with treatment-naïve type I AIH by single-cell spatial transcriptomics to identify cellular and signalling features that distinguish the two conditions and could inform second-line treatment.

Four formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) liver biopsies were profiled. The two SR-irHep cases (18321_a, 24774_d) received neoadjuvant carboplatin–paclitaxel–pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-522 regimen) for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC); hepatitis emerged during the concurrent chemotherapy + ICI phase in 18321_a and after chemotherapy completion, during pembrolizumab maintenance, in 24774_d. Both biopsies were taken at the clinical decision to escalate to second-line immunosuppression after failed corticosteroid response (failure of clinical or biochemical improvement at ≥ 1 mg/kg/day prednisone-equivalent^7,8^; SITC consensus irAE terminology^9^). The two AIH type I cases (54023_b, 56784_c) were biopsied treatment-naïve at diagnosis. Per-patient demographics, treatment timelines and histology are in **Supplementary Table S1**. Prior to study participation all patients provided written informed consent. All procedures followed the policies of the ethics committee of Regione Liguria (Genoa, Italy) in the matter of publication of case reports and/or case series. 10× Xenium *in situ* spatial transcriptomics was performed as a vendor service with the 380-gene Immuno-Oncology Panel; after QC, 146,014 cells across the four biopsies were retained (all samples passed vendor reference ranges; per-sample QC metrics in **Supplementary Table S2**).

Integrated Leiden clustering (Harmony on the first 15 principal components, resolution 0.5)^10^ yielded 21 primary clusters. Three were excluded after pathology-aware review (cluster 21, four-cell outlier; cluster 12, fragment cluster (median area ≤ 20 µm^2^) with IGKC as sole marker; cluster 15, hepatocyte-marker cluster in poorly-preserved regions), leaving **18 active clusters spanning the major hepatic and immune cell populations**: five hepatocyte states (including a proliferating MKI67/CDK1+ pool and an Ig⁺ hepatocyte subset excluded from the Figure 2B display), three CD8⁺ subclusters (CD8T1–3), one CD4⁺ cluster, cholangiocytes, IgM⁺ and IgG⁺ B and plasmablast subsets, two macrophage states, and three stromal/vascular populations (Fig. 1A,B; full cluster definitions, markers, fold-changes and per-sample counts in **Supplementary Note 3** and **Supplementary Tables S3, S4**).

Per-condition composition (Fig. 1A, lower bars; per-sample-averaged) was hepatocyte-dominated in SR-irHep (Hep1–3 totalling 50.8% vs 18.0% in AIH) and immune-rich in AIH, where each CD8⁺ subcluster (CD8T1 16.0% vs 6.6%; CD8T2 2.4% vs 0.8%; CD8T3 1.1% vs 0.8%), CD4⁺ T cells (6.1% vs 4.3%), IgG⁺ plasmablasts (5.6% vs 0.4%), IgM⁺ B cells (3.1% vs 1.2%) and both macrophage states (Macrophage 1: 11.3% vs 7.9%; Macrophage 2: 9.5% vs 6.3%) occupied a larger fraction of tissue than in SR-irHep. Cholangiocytes were also a smaller fraction in SR-irHep (1.7%) than in AIH (5.3%). The contrast is hepatocyte-dominated, cholangiocyte-reduced, infiltrate-sparse SR-irHep versus infiltrate-rich, lymphoplasmacytic AIH, consistent with bile-duct injury as the histological correlate. Both SR-irHep biopsies show bile-duct injury on standard histology, with the reduced cholangiocyte fraction as its quantitative correlate. Frank ductopenia (loss of interlobular bile ducts in >50% of portal tracts) was not present in these two samples, consistent with a lesion sampled before the vanishing-bile-duct endpoint that characterises the progressive subset^4^. The lower immune fraction in SR-irHep should be read under the steroid-exposed sampling context: corticosteroids suppress the parenchymal lymphocytic infiltrate, whereas the cholangitic/bile-duct compartment of checkpoint-inhibitor liver injury responds poorly to corticosteroids^11^, so the sparse residual immune signal does not exclude immune-mediated pathogenesis.

Tissue-level overviews of one representative sample per condition (Fig. 1C, SR-irHep sample 24774_d; Fig. 1D, AIH sample 54023_b) render the cluster identities at the segmentation level. Per-condition 600 × 600 µm ROIs show that the dominant immune compartment occupies distinct anatomical sites: the SR-irHep ROI shows bile-duct injury with an interface CD4/CD8 infiltrate, while the AIH ROI shows tertiary-lymphoid-structure (TLS)-like B/plasma aggregates with parenchymal depletion.

To resolve the spatial geometry of the immune infiltrates quantitatively, we computed pairwise cluster adjacency per sample by Delaunay-graph proximity enrichment (1,000 label permutations) and aggregated to a per-condition coherent signal (Methods). The 10 pairs with largest coherent |SR-irHep − AIH| Δlog₂ proximity segregated anatomically (Fig. 2A): in SR-irHep, all three CD8⁺ subclusters sat closer to cholangiocytes than in AIH, with IFN-γ-stimulated CD8T2 (Δlog₂ enrichment +2.75) and proliferating CD8T3 (+1.83) occupying the top-ranked duct-engaged pairs; in AIH, CD4⁺ T cells sat closer to all four hepatocyte subclusters than in SR-irHep (Δlog₂ −1.4 to −2.8 across Hep1–4). The anatomical site of effector–target engagement therefore inverts between the two conditions: cholangiocyte-centred in SR-irHep, hepatocyte-centred in AIH.

We next inferred ligand–receptor (L–R) communication per sample with CellChat v2 within a 25 µm spatial neighbourhood^12^, splitting multi-ligand families (MHC-I, CXCL, CCL) into receptor-specific axes (Methods). The coherent L–R × cell-pair flows with the largest absolute log₂ fold-change are shown in Fig. 2B: 8 SR-irHep-enriched and 10 AIH-enriched (up to 10 per direction). SR-irHep-enriched cholangiocyte-origin flows centred on the bile-duct–CD8 architecture: MHC-I antigen presentation by cholangiocytes to CD8T2 and CD8T3 via the CD8A/CD8B α/β co-receptor chains (log₂FC +8.2 and +9.0 respectively, the strongest SR-irHep-biased axes); VEGFA–FLT1 onto CD8T2; and CXCL9/10/11–CXCR3 chemoattraction of CD8T3. CXCL9/10/11 are canonical IFN-γ-inducible chemokines (induced via JAK1/2–STAT1) that signal through CXCR3^13^; SR-irHep cholangiocytes show an elevated IFN-response composite (STAT1, IRF1, IDO1, ISG15, MX1) co-expressed at single-cell level with CXCL9/10/11, providing transcriptional support for the IFN-γ-driven framing without identifying the IFN-γ source (IFNG is not on the panel; Supplementary Fig. S5, Tables S11c, S11c2 and S11c3). A separate SR-irHep-enriched vascular-endothelial flow signalled onto CD8T2 through CCL3/4/5–CCR1, paralleling central-vein endotheliitis previously described in anti-CTLA-4 ICI hepatitis^14^ (our cases are PD-1-blockade-derived, where this endotheliitis pattern is less established). AIH-enriched flows were directed at parenchymal CD4 axes, with hepatocyte and sinusoidal-endothelial sources predominating: sinusoidal endothelium to CD4 via CD86 costimulation (established in inflamed liver biology) and CCL/CCR4 chemoattraction; Hep2 to CD4 via CCL3/4/5–CCR1 chemoattraction and ICOSLG to CD28 costimulation (CellChat "ICOS" pathway); Hep1/Hep2 to CD4 via CXCL chemokines and CXCR3/CXCR6; and CD4 to Hep2 via CD40LG–CD40. A single AIH-enriched cholangiocyte to CD8T2 fibronectin (FN1) flow was the strongest AIH-biased interaction, consistent with chronic matrix deposition at the duct surface. LIANA+ five-method consensus^15^ and COMMOT spatial optimal transport at 250 µm diffusion^16^ showed partial, lineage-level concordance with the dominant bile-duct-CD8 and parenchymal-CD4 architectures, though the MHC-I and CXCR3 triples were not themselves confirmed by all three methods (Supplementary §1.1, §2.1; per-method tables **Supplementary Tables S5–S6**; COMMOT vector visualisation **Supplementary Fig. S1**).

Four 120 × 120 µm ROIs (two anatomy rows × two conditions; Methods) are rendered as paired H&E and Xenium segmentation overlays (Fig. 2C). SR-irHep cholangiocytes are encircled by a dense CD8T2/CD8T3 ring; AIH bile ducts are cholangiocyte-dense but show markedly sparser CD8 contacts per duct cell. The parenchymal row shows the inverse pattern: in SR-irHep, CD4⁺ T cells lie largely outside hepatocyte plates, whereas in the AIH parenchymal ROI CD4⁺ cells are apposed to hepatocytes with IL7R⁺ transcripts concentrating at the interface.

Across proximity, communication and tissue scales, the two conditions differ along one axis. The site of effector–target apposition inverts (bile-duct-centred in SR-irHep, parenchyma-centred in AIH), and so does the dominant effector lineage: CD8⁺ T cells apposed to cholangiocytes in SR-irHep, despite their lower overall abundance than in AIH, matching the antitumour-like CD8 cytotoxic machinery known to damage epithelial bystanders under ICI (most directly characterised in ICI-related thyroiditis^17^), against CD4⁺ T cells engaged with hepatocytes in AIH. The inferred signalling interactions follow the same split: MHC-I antigen presentation and putatively IFN-γ-driven CXCR3 chemoattraction at the cholangiocyte surface in SR-irHep, against costimulation (CD86, ICOSLG–CD28, CD40LG–CD40) and broad chemokine signalling (CCR1/4, CXCR3/6) in AIH. These findings extend the histological observations of Sunago^4^ (bile-duct injury linked to steroid refractoriness) and Bjornsson^5^ (predominant CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in ICI sclerosing cholangitis biopsies) by adding cell–cell signalling axes that point to candidate targets.

The cholangiocyte–CD8/MHC-I/CXCR3 architecture frames a clinical observation about MMF and motivates two second-line hypotheses. MMF preferentially inhibits IMPDH in activated, de-novo-purine-dependent lymphocytes^18^, including the cycling MKI67/CDK1-high CD8T3 subset engaged with cholangiocytes at the SR-irHep bile-duct interface (Fig. 2A), providing a plausible cellular basis in these biopsies for its empirical second-line use. Among the off-label second-line options used in SR-irHep (tacrolimus, ATG, tocilizumab, vedolizumab and others^7^; infliximab^19,20^ and ruxolitinib^21,22^ in more recent case reports), ruxolitinib intersects our findings most directly: JAK1/2 inhibition would attenuate the IFN-γ to JAK1/2–STAT1 to CXCL9/10/11 to CXCR3 axis at the bile-duct surface^13^. Two case-level reports (SR-irHep tertiary rescue after corticosteroid and tocilizumab failure^21^ and a Triple-M overlap case^22^) provide initial but limited documentation of clinical use, not established efficacy. CXCR3-pathway antagonism emerges as a second observation-driven hypothesis for second-line targeting: blockade attenuated ICI-related myocarditis in murine and human single-cell datasets, with antitumour immunity preserved in the preclinical model^23^. CXCR3-pathway signalling features in flows enriched in both conditions; the discriminator from AIH is cellular geometry: cholangiocyte to CD8 in SR-irHep versus hepatocyte to CD4 in AIH. The target that distinguishes the two conditions is CD8 engagement of cholangiocytes. Translation faces constraints: systemic CXCR3 inhibition during active immunotherapy could compromise antitumour immunity (CXCR3/CXCL9–11 sustains anti-PD-1 efficacy via CD8 recruitment^24^ and intratumoural NK infiltration in liver metastases^25^), so organ-localised or temporally restricted strategies would be needed; we are not aware of a clinically validated CXCR3 antagonist in oncology use; ruxolitinib carries infection (HBV reactivation, VZV, TB), cytopenia and drug-drug-interaction risks^26^. Protein-level and functional blockade readouts would be required before clinical translation. We propose the cholangiocyte–CD8/CXCR3 axis as a spatial-transcriptomic candidate target for SR-irHep, meriting dedicated preclinical evaluation alongside the randomised SR-irAE second-line programmes currently being launched in adjacent indications (e.g., PHOENIX-ECP^27^).

Several limitations bound the present study. With n = 2 per condition, analyses are descriptive of observed biopsies, not population-inferential; independent-cohort replication is mandatory before clinical translation. The cohort trades sample size for single-cell spatial resolution, complementing larger histology-based studies of ICI-related liver injury^4,5^ at the cell-class, not molecular-axis, level. The comparator is not disease-matched: SR-irHep received neoadjuvant carboplatin–paclitaxel–pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-522) for TNBC and are steroid-exposed at biopsy; AIH are treatment-naïve and tumour-free with distinct pathology; observed differences could reflect any combination, and the 18321_a chemo-overlap window adds a drug-attribution confound (though paclitaxel/platinum hepatobiliary injury is more commonly cholestatic than the cytotoxic CD8/bile-duct architecture seen here). The properly matched comparator (steroid-responsive ICI hepatitis, matched for ICI exposure, cancer and corticosteroid history) is unavailable, so treatment-naïve AIH serves as a non-matched benchmark of immune-mediated hepatic inflammation absent ICI exposure; convergence with prior histology^4,5^ and 80% pembrolizumab predominance among ICI-related cholangiopathy cases in a recent multi-centre series^28^ argues against pure treatment-history artefact. Panel coverage further constrains within-lineage resolution: the 380-gene panel includes canonical exhaustion and tissue-resident-memory markers (per-cluster expression in Supplementary Table S11) but did not drive discrete exhausted-CD8 or TRM-CD8 subclusters at this cohort size and Leiden resolution (CD8T2 CXCR6+ and CD8T3 cycling, MKI67/CDK1-high span only part of this spectrum); finer T-helper identity (Th1/Th17/Tfh/Tph), IL21-driven CD4 help and peripheral CD8 clonality are unresolved; KRT7/KRT19 and hepatocyte-zonation markers are absent, so cholangiocyte identity rests on EPCAM/SOX9/CXCL6 and hepatocytes are reported without zonal assignment; these gaps reflect a panel-design trade-off, addressable on emerging higher-plex or transcriptome-wide spatial platforms (Visium HD, Stereo-seq, Curio Seeker, MERSCOPE 1k-plex) in follow-up. TCR clonality, peripheral phenotype and protein-level cytokine measurements are outside the scope of this spatial transcriptomic study. The reported flows are spatial signalling architectures, not mechanistic demonstrations: ligand–receptor and cytokine inferences are computational predictions from a targeted panel at the transcript level, their direct demonstration would require deeper-transcriptome or functional readouts beyond a 380-gene panel, and orthogonal protein-level validation of the inferred axes is required before mechanistic claims. The bile-duct CD8/MHC-I/CXCR3 architecture provides a spatial description of the SR-irHep subset, offers a cellular rationale for the currently employed second-line agents such as MMF and ruxolitinib, and suggests CXCR3 antagonism as a second-line candidate; independent-cohort validation, immunohistochemical or multiplex-protein confirmation of the principal axes, and preclinical functional studies in cholangiocyte–CD8 co-culture or organoid systems are warranted.

# Methods

## Patients, samples and ethics

Liver biopsies from four patients were profiled at the Azienda Ospedaliera Metropolitana IRCCS Ospedale Policlinico San Martino (Genoa, Italy) as a retrospective small case series: two patients with steroid-resistant immune-checkpoint-inhibitor (ICI) hepatitis (SR-irHep; sample identifiers 18321_a, 24774_d) on neoadjuvant carboplatin–paclitaxel–pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-522 regimen) for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), meeting the operational definition of steroid-refractory irHep (failure of clinical or biochemical improvement after corticosteroid therapy at ≥ 1 mg/kg/day prednisone-equivalent^7,8^), and two patients with treatment-naïve autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) type I, diagnosed clinically and serologically per international AIH simplified criteria (sample identifiers 54023_b, 56784_c). Per-patient regimen timing, steroid history at biopsy, post-biopsy second-line therapy and biopsy-level histology are tabulated in Supplementary Table S1. Sample identifiers are pseudonymous codes; the linkage to the source patient is held only at the institutional level. All four patients had provided written informed consent for the research use and publication of de-identified clinical and biological material under the institutional research-consent framework in place at the Azienda Ospedaliera Metropolitana IRCCS Ospedale Policlinico San Martino. Under Italian regulations on retrospective observational research at IRCCS institutions (AIFA Determina 20 March 2008; Decreto Ministeriale 30 November 2021; DGR Liguria 858/2023), a dedicated retrospective study protocol was not required for this small case series; all procedures followed the policies of the ethics committee of Regione Liguria in the matter of publication of case reports and/or case series. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) as implemented in Italian law (D.Lgs. 196/2003 as amended by D.Lgs. 101/2018). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were sectioned at 5 µm onto Xenium slides per the 10× Genomics manufacturer's protocol.

## Xenium spatial transcriptomics

*In situ* profiling was performed on the 10× Genomics Xenium Analyzer with the 380-gene Human Immuno-Oncology Panel. Standard vendor protocols were followed for slide preparation, hybridisation, decoding, and cell segmentation (cell + nuclear boundaries from 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) plus interior cell-membrane staining). After Xenium acquisition, slides were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and the resulting whole-slide images were registered to the Xenium coordinate space using the vendor `he_alignment/matrix.csv` affine transform; per-ROI alignment was visually verified within the 10 µm contact threshold used in Figure 2C. Per-sample output (cell-feature matrix, transcripts, cell and nucleus boundaries, DAPI and H&E pyramids, gene panel JSON) was retained in 10× standard layout. Per-sample QC metrics are reported in Supplementary Table S2; all four samples passed vendor reference ranges.

## Data processing, integration and clustering

Per-sample cell-feature matrices were imported into the Giotto framework (v4.2.2). Cell-level QC retained cells with at least 4 transcripts; genes were retained if detected in at least 3 cells; transcripts with decoding quality QV < 20 were discarded. Counts were normalised by log-transformed library-size scaling (scale factor 5,000). The four samples were embedded with principal-component analysis (PCA; 50 components computed), batch-corrected with Harmony (theta = 2, sigma = 0.1, lambda = 1) on the first 15 principal components, projected with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP; 15 nearest neighbours, min_dist = 0.0005), and clustered with the Leiden algorithm (k = 15, resolution 0.5). Twenty-one primary clusters were obtained. Three were excluded after pathology-aware quality review: one four-cell sample-specific outlier (cluster 21); a fragment cluster with median cell area ≤ 20 µm^2^ and IGKC as the dominant detected marker, consistent with segmentation artefacts (cluster 12); and a hepatocyte-marker cluster restricted to a poorly preserved tissue region (cluster 15). Eighteen active clusters across 146,014 cells were retained. Cluster identity was assigned from per-cluster marker genes (Giotto `findMarkers_one_vs_all`, scran method, FDR < 0.05) against canonical liver-atlas signatures. Full top-5 markers and per-sample cell counts are in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4; per-marker specificity statistics are in Supplementary Table S8.

## Per-cluster marker-gene identification

For cluster characterisation, per-cluster vs. all-other-clusters differential expression was computed with Giotto's wrapper around `scran::findMarkers` on per-cell log-normalised counts (Supplementary Table S3). The top markers per cluster were used to assign cluster identity against canonical liver and immune-cell signatures; top-5 markers and per-sample cell counts are tabulated in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4.

## Spatial proximity analysis (Figure 2A)

Pairwise cluster–cluster adjacency was quantified per sample with `Giotto::cellProximityEnrichment` on a Delaunay spatial graph of cell centroids. The enrichment statistic is log₂((observed + 1) / (expected + 1)), where the expected number of neighbours is drawn from 1,000 random permutations of cluster labels within each sample. Two one-sided empirical p-values per pair (attract, avoid) were computed and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted within sample. To accommodate the small cohort size (n = 2 per condition), a *coherent rule* was applied per direction per condition: a pair was retained when at least one sample of the condition reached padj < 0.10 in a given direction *and* no sample of the same condition crossed padj < 0.10 in the opposite direction. The condition mean log₂ enrichment was computed across the two samples of each condition; the differential Δ(SR-irHep − AIH) was used as tile colour in Figure 2A. The 10 pairs with largest |Δ| under the coherent rule are outlined in the heatmap. A permutation-based supplementary analysis (1,000 cluster-label permutations across the four samples; Supplementary Table S7) was conducted to confirm that the coherent signal is unlikely to arise by chance. Because Figure 2A and Supplementary Table S7 use different estimators (per-condition cellProximityEnrichment mean vs four-sample permutation), the two sets of Δlog₂ values agree in direction and rank but differ in absolute magnitude by design.

## Cell–cell communication (Figure 2B)

Per-sample ligand–receptor (L–R) communication probabilities were inferred with CellChat v2^12^ using a spatial neighbourhood (`contact.range` = 25 µm, `spot.size` = 10, `type = "truncatedMean"`, `trim` = 0.1) against the full CellChatDB. Three multi-ligand families whose CellChat defaults collapse mechanistically distinct receptors (MHC-I, CXCL and CCL) were ligand-split into receptor-specific axes (e.g. MHC-I_CD8A, CXCL_CXCR3, CCL_CCR1) before aggregation, yielding the receptor-anchored pathway labels shown in the Sankey. For the MHC-I family, every nucleated cell expresses HLA-A/B/C, so CellChat's source-is-ligand-expresser convention can place a CD8⁺ cluster in the source column on multi-cell-type comparisons; in MHC-I rows with a CD8 cluster as source, we swap source and target so the row reads presenter to CD8 (full description in Supplementary Note 2). Per-condition mean communication probabilities were log₂-transformed with a pseudo-count and the differential Δ(SR-irHep − AIH) computed. The same coherent rule used in the proximity analysis was applied to the L–R × cell-pair triple: a flow was retained as enriched in a condition when at least one sample of that condition reached a CellChat permutation p < 0.05 for the triple and no sample of the opposite condition did (a between-sample agreement filter, not a per-triple significance test; the separate Fisher-combined, Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted multiplicity assessment over the coherent set is reported in Supplementary §1.5 and Table S10). Forty-seven coherent triples passed; 45 remained after the n ≥ 10 per-sample display filter (which removes two Ig⁺-hepatocyte flows). Of these, the 18 flows with the largest |log₂FC|, 8 SR-irHep-enriched and 10 AIH-enriched (up to 10 per direction), are shown in Figure 2B, with clusters of < 10 cells in any sample excluded. The full coherent set is in Supplementary Table S5.
## Orthogonal validation: LIANA+ and COMMOT

CellChat findings were cross-referenced against two independent computational approaches operating on the same per-sample data. *Expression-only consensus.* Per-sample expression-only communication scoring was performed with LIANA+^15^ using five orthogonal scoring methods (NATMI, Connectome, logFC, SingleCellSignalR and CellPhoneDB) against the LIANA Consensus database with Robust Rank Aggregation; cross-method per-pair ranks are in Supplementary Table S6. *Spatial optimal transport.* Per-sample spatial-aware CCC was computed with COMMOT^16^ using the CellChatDB ligand–receptor database, species = "human", cell-cell distance threshold `dis_thr` = 250 µm and heteromeric receptor handling enabled. COMMOT was run on each sample individually (the largest sample, 54023_b, with 52,266 cells × 33 testable L–R pairs, was the dominant computational cost). Per-sample scores were aggregated to per-condition differentials with explicit three-layer handling of structural missingness (presence-only versus testable-zero versus untested); details are in the Supplementary Methods.

## Representative regions of interest (Figure 2C)

Four representative 120 × 120 µm regions of interest (ROIs) were rendered to ground the cohort-level findings in tissue: two anatomy rows (bile-duct, parenchymal) × two condition columns. ROIs were selected by pathology review of the four sections as representative of each condition × anatomy axis (bile-duct ROIs centred on a duct surrounded by lymphocytic infiltrate; parenchymal ROIs centred on a hepatocyte-dense field with lymphocytes in apposition). Coordinates of the selected ROIs (within each tissue, in Xenium image coordinates): bile-duct row, SR-irHep sample 18321_a (centre 1572, −2146) and AIH sample 54023_b (3195, −9694); parenchymal row, SR-irHep sample 18321_a (8228, −1175) and AIH sample 54023_b (3207, −9960). Each row highlights the anatomy and its primary Panel-B partner: bile-duct row, cholangiocytes (gold) and the unified Chol-engaged CD8 subclusters CD8T2 + CD8T3 (medium/dark blue); parenchymal row, hepatocytes (red, four hepatocyte subclusters unified) and CD4⁺ T cells (mint). All other clusters appear as light-grey "Other cells". Cell and nucleus boundaries were taken from the Xenium `cell_boundaries.parquet` and `nucleus_boundaries.parquet` files; cluster identity per polygon was assigned by nearest-centroid match to the Giotto cell-feature object. Partner-lineage cells whose centroid lay within 10 µm of any anatomy-partner cell-boundary vertex were flagged "in contact"; these cells received a magenta border and a jittered in-pane arrow pointing at them from outside the cell (yellow on H&E, white on the segmentation overlay). Xenium-aligned H&E images were cropped per ROI by an affine-registration pipeline using the vendor `he_alignment/matrix.csv`, with contrast-normalisation applied identically across all ROIs. Cell-type-specific transcripts were filtered for genes (excluding control probes) at QV ≥ 20 and plotted as single-molecule dots within the ROI box without cell-membership filtering, so gene–cell specificity is visible directly. Genes shown: EPCAM, CXCL6 (cholangiocyte markers) and CD8A, KLRK1 (CD8 T-cell markers) on the bile-duct row; ARG1 (hepatocyte marker) and IL7R, CD2 (CD4 T-cell markers) on the parenchymal row. Panel C is a visual illustration of the cohort-level proximity (Fig. 2A) and ligand–receptor (Fig. 2B) findings at single-cell scale; the ROIs were selected to illustrate cohort-level results, not to generate the statistics shown in Panels A and B.

## Software, data and code availability

All analyses were performed in R 4.5.2 (Giotto 4.2.2, GiottoClass, CellChat 2.x, scran, ggalluvial, ggplot2, cowplot, magick, FNN, arrow, data.table) and Python 3.10+ (commot 0.0.3, anndata, tifffile, scipy.ndimage, PIL). Conda environment specifications for both languages and an end-to-end pipeline launcher are deposited with the analysis repository. Random seeds are fixed for the Leiden clustering, the proximity-permutation step and ROI arrow jitter; all other steps are deterministic.
